# Bruce Museum demo: audio scripts

Four stops, about 75 seconds each spoken (~180 words), written for the ear: short
sentences, and something to look for in each painting. For an AI voice (ElevenLabs,
OpenAI TTS) or a human read. Written 2026-10-08 from auction catalogue records and
the Bruce's 2022 press release. **Nobody has checked the scripts against the
Bruce's own images yet**: confirm every "look at…" line matches the actual canvas
before recording.

Tour thread: Pissarro's French market town → Cassatt, his friend, in the same
countryside → Sargent, the American abroad → Hassam, the American who came home,
and ended up just down the road in Cos Cob.

# Short versions (about 45–50 s each, ~110 words), the ones to record

Informative, not conversational: no questions, no "look at…", no heart prompts.

## 1. Camille Pissarro, *The Market of Gisors, Grande-Rue* (1885)

You're looking at *The Market of Gisors, Grande-Rue*, painted by Camille Pissarro in 1885.

In 1884 Pissarro settled in Éragny, a village a few miles from the Normandy market town of Gisors. On market days he sketched the farmers and traders gathered on the Grande-Rue, near the town hall.

Pissarro is best known for landscapes, but he returned again and again to the people who worked the land. Between 1880 and 1901 he made around three dozen market scenes, almost all in gouache or pastel. This is one of only five he painted in oil on canvas.

The crowded, unposed composition reflects his political convictions: ordinary working people as worthy subjects for art.

## 2. Mary Cassatt, *Two Little Sisters* (about 1901–02)

This is *Two Little Sisters*, by Mary Cassatt, painted around 1901.

Cassatt was born near Pittsburgh in 1844 and settled in Paris in 1874. At Edgar Degas's invitation she exhibited with the Impressionists, the only American to do so. She worked closely with Degas and Camille Pissarro, especially on printmaking.

From 1894 she lived at a country house northwest of Paris, where local children often served as her models. Cassatt never married or had children, but children became her central subject.

Here the paint is thickest on the faces and hair; the clothing is suggested in a few loose strokes. The painting was once owned by Ambroise Vollard, the Paris dealer who championed Cézanne and Picasso.

## 3. John Singer Sargent, *Girl Fishing* (1913)

Here is *Girl Fishing*, by John Singer Sargent, from 1913.

Sargent, born in Florence to American parents in 1856, was the most sought-after portrait painter of his generation. By 1907 he had largely given up painted portraits, turning instead to landscapes and scenes of daily life painted outdoors.

In September 1913 Sargent, his sister Emily and a group of friends stayed at San Vigilio, on the shore of Lake Garda in northern Italy. He painted the lake, its boats and cypress trees, and his companions at leisure.

This was not a commission. The quick, confident handling of water and reflected light shows Sargent painting for himself.

## 4. Childe Hassam, *Rainy Day on the Avenue* (1893)

This is *Rainy Day on the Avenue*, painted by Childe Hassam in 1893.

Hassam was born near Boston in 1859. He studied in Paris from 1886 to 1889, where he absorbed the methods of the French Impressionists, then moved to New York.

There he painted the city's streets in every kind of weather. Rain, snow and dusk softened outlines and filled the pavement with reflections, effects he returned to throughout the early 1890s.

From 1896 Hassam spent many summers in Cos Cob, a few miles from this museum, at the center of an artists' colony. The Bruce Museum's first exhibition, in 1912, featured the Greenwich Society of Artists, and their work formed the core of its American Impressionist collection.

# Long versions (~75 s)

## 1. Camille Pissarro, *The Market of Gisors, Grande-Rue* (1885)

Stand back a step. You're on the main street of Gisors, a market town northwest of Paris, in 1885.

Camille Pissarro lived a few miles away, in the village of Éragny. On market days he made the trip into town with a sketchbook, and drew the crowds from the farms and villages around.

He's best known for landscapes: fields, orchards, open sky. But all his life he kept coming back to the people who worked that land. First he painted them in the fields. Here, he follows them into town, where they sell what they've grown.

Look at how crowded it is. No one is posing, and there's no single hero. Just people, shoulder to shoulder, bargaining and talking.

That was deliberate. Pissarro held strong political convictions, and he believed working people deserved a place on a gallery wall.

He drew market scenes like this dozens of times, but painted only a handful in oil on canvas. This is one of them.

If you'd like to come back to this one, tap the heart. Then, wherever you like, find the next painting.

## 2. Mary Cassatt, *Two Little Sisters* (c. 1901–02)

Two girls, pressed close together. One leans into the other. Look at their cheeks, and their golden hair: that's where the paint is thickest. The clothes are only sketched in, a few quick strokes. Cassatt wanted your eye on the faces.

Mary Cassatt was an American from Pennsylvania who made her career in Paris. She was the only American invited to show with the French Impressionists. Degas brought her in, and Pissarro became a friend. Around 1880 the three of them worked together on printmaking.

By the time she painted this, Cassatt was living in the French countryside northwest of Paris, the same region as Pissarro's market town. Her models were usually local children, not her own family. She never married, and never had children.

Children were her great subject. Not sweet, posed portraits, but children as they actually are: restless, affectionate, a little bored with sitting still.

Look again at the two sisters. Which one do you think is older?

Tap the heart to save this one, and move on whenever you're ready.

## 3. John Singer Sargent, *Girl Fishing* (1913)

Late summer, 1913. Lake Garda, in the north of Italy.

John Singer Sargent was the most famous portrait painter of his day. Presidents, duchesses, and millionaires all wanted a Sargent. And by 1913 he'd had enough. He joked to a friend that he was finished with "paughtraits," and spelled it that way, on purpose.

So he went on holiday. A group of family and friends took over most of a small inn at San Vigilio, on the shore of the lake, and Sargent painted whatever caught his eye. Boats, cypress trees, and his companions, idling by the water.

That's what this is. Not a commission. Nobody paid for it, and nobody told him how to pose her. Just someone fishing, on a warm afternoon.

Look at the light, and the water. Sargent could paint a reflection in a few strokes that look careless up close and exactly right from where you're standing.

Step closer, then back again, and watch it happen.

Tap the heart if this is one you'd like to come back to.

---

## 4. Childe Hassam, *Rainy Day on the Avenue* (1893)

New York, 1893. It's raining.

Look at the street. It's wet enough to hold reflections, of carriages, of people, of the buildings above. Hassam loved weather. Rain, snow, mist: anything that softened the edges of a city.

Childe Hassam was born outside Boston and trained in Paris, where he saw the Impressionists at work. He came home and asked a simple question: what would that kind of painting look like in an American city? Paintings like this are his answer.

He once said that nothing interested him as much as people. Look how many there are here, and how few of them you can actually make out. Just shapes under umbrellas, hurrying somewhere.

A few years after this, Hassam found another place to paint: Cos Cob, just a few miles from where you're standing. He spent many summers there, and the artists' colony that grew up around him helped shape this museum. The Bruce's very first exhibition, in 1912, was of Greenwich artists.

So this one has come home.

Tap the heart to save it. Thanks for listening.
